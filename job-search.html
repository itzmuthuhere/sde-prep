Job Search — The Operating System

Studying is one loop; getting hired is a different one, and it runs in parallel. This page is the machine for that loop: a resume that passes the six-second scan, a LinkedIn profile recruiters actually find, a repeatable way to build a target list, outreach that gets replies, and the trackers that keep 40 open threads from turning into chaos — through negotiation and picking the right offer.

How to use this page. Work it top to bottom once to set everything up (resume, LinkedIn, target list, templates, trackers), then it becomes a weekly dashboard: add companies, fire outreach, log every interview stage and the exact questions asked, and review the pipeline every Sunday. The three tables (targets, applications, pipeline) are real <table> elements — open this file in an editor and duplicate a <tr> row to add your own. The checklist at the bottom of each section tracks setup, not applications.

1. Resume — built for the six-second scan

A recruiter spends about six seconds on the first pass and an ATS parses it before that. So the resume has one job: make it obvious in one screen that you're a backend engineer who ships. One page, single column (multi-column layouts confuse ATS parsers), a standard font, no photo, no skill bars, PDF named Firstname_Lastname_Backend_Engineer.pdf. Section order for someone with 2–6 years of experience: Summary → Skills → Experience → Projects → Education. Experience is 70% of the page.

Every bullet is an accomplishment, not a responsibility. The formula: action verb → what you built → the technical how → the measured result. If you can't measure it, quantify the scope instead (requests/day, rows, services, team size). "Responsible for maintaining APIs" is invisible. "Cut p99 latency on the checkout API from 900 ms to 180 ms by adding a read-through Redis cache and fixing an N+1 query, serving 2M requests/day" is a conversation.

Core intuition

The reader is pattern-matching against a job description. Mirror its language: if the JD says "distributed systems," "Kafka," "observability," those exact words should appear in your bullets (truthfully). Tailor the Summary and reorder skills per application — that's 10 minutes and it moves you past keyword filters and the six-second scan.

Pre-send checklist

  • One page. One column. Selectable text (open the PDF, Ctrl+A — if nothing highlights, the ATS sees nothing).
  • Contact line: name, city, phone, professional email, LinkedIn URL, GitHub URL. No full address.
  • Summary is 2–3 lines, role-specific, no "hardworking team player."
  • Skills grouped: Languages / Frameworks / Data & Infra / Practices. Only list what you can be grilled on.
  • Every experience bullet starts with a past-tense verb (Built, Led, Migrated, Reduced, Designed).
  • Each role has at least one bullet with a hard number.
  • No pronouns, no articles at the start of bullets, consistent tense and punctuation.
  • Most impressive / most relevant bullet is the first bullet of your most recent role.
  • 2–3 projects with a one-line "what + stack + link." GitHub links actually resolve.
  • Dates aligned right, format consistent (Mar 2022 – Present). No unexplained gaps.
  • Ran it through jobscan or a JD keyword diff for the specific role.
  • Spell-checked. Someone else read it. Exported fresh PDF (not "Save As" from an old one — re-embeds fonts).

Before → after: one bullet

Before
  • Worked on the notification system and was responsible for its reliability.
  • Used Kafka for messaging between services.
  • Helped improve database performance.
After
  • Re-architected the notification service to a queue-per-channel model with exponential backoff and a dead-letter queue, raising successful delivery from 94% to 99.7% across ~1.2M messages/day.
  • Introduced an outbox pattern (Postgres → Kafka poller) to remove a dual-write bug that was dropping ~200 events/day during broker failovers.
  • Cut the orders list endpoint from 1.4 s to 210 ms by replacing an N+1 fetch with a single join projection and adding a covering index, verified with Testcontainers load tests.

What changed: a specific action verb, the mechanism (queue-per-channel, outbox, covering index), and a number that frames the scope. Each "after" bullet is a door into a 10-minute technical story you can defend.

2. LinkedIn optimization

Recruiters search LinkedIn with boolean queries against your headline, About, skills, and job titles. Ranking favours completeness ("All-Star" profile), keyword match, and recent activity. You want to show up for "backend engineer" AND (Java OR Spring) AND (Kafka OR microservices) and look active when they click.

Headline formula

Not just your job title. Use: Role · Core stack · Domain / impact (220 chars, front-load the keywords).

Examples
  • Backend Engineer · Java, Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Kafka · Building payment & order systems at scale
  • Software Engineer (Backend) | Distributed systems, microservices, AWS | 3+ yrs shipping high-throughput APIs
  • SDE II · Java / Spring / Kubernetes · Reliability & performance for e-commerce backends

Featured section (3 items)

  • Your best GitHub project repo — the one with a real README and an architecture diagram.
  • A short write-up: a blog post, a Gist, or a PDF of a design doc you can share. Even one "how I built X" post signals more than a dozen endorsements.
  • Your resume PDF, or a link to a portfolio / the deployed project.

Profile checklist

  • Photo: plain background, face fills ~60% of frame. Banner: simple, not a stock skyline (a subtle tech pattern or your city is fine).
  • Custom URL: linkedin.com/in/firstname-lastname.
  • "Open to work" set to recruiters only (green badge is optional; recruiters-only avoids signalling to your current employer).
  • Location set to your target metro if you'll relocate.
  • Every past role has 2–3 accomplishment bullets (reuse resume bullets).
  • Skills: pin the top 3 to match your headline; get a few endorsements on those.
  • Turn on "notify network of job changes" only after you start a new role, not during the search.
  • Activity: comment thoughtfully on 2–3 engineering posts a week, or repost with a sentence of your take. Recency feeds ranking.
  • Request 2 recommendations from people you worked closely with; offer to write theirs first.

3. Target company research method

Spray-and-pray applications convert at roughly 1–2%. A researched, referred application converts many times better. The method below is a repeatable ~15-minute-per-company pipeline that produces a ranked target list and the raw material for outreach.

Step 1 — Build the raw list (cast wide)

  • Company career pages — the source of truth for openings and titles. Bookmark 20–30 and check weekly; roles often appear here days before job boards.
  • LinkedIn Jobs with search operators in the keyword box:
    ("backend engineer" OR "software engineer") AND (java OR spring) NOT (senior staff OR principal OR intern)
    Filter: Date posted → past week, Experience level, On-site/Hybrid/Remote.
  • LinkedIn people search to find who to contact: title:("engineering manager" OR "recruiter") AND "CompanyName", then filter by 2nd-degree connections.
  • Wellfound (AngelList) — startups, often direct-to-founder applications, salary + equity shown up front.
  • Aggregators — a curated newsletter or two in your niche, company lists (YC, well-funded Series B+), and levels.fyi to see which companies pay in your target band.
  • Your network — ex-colleagues, college seniors, people from your city. A warm intro beats everything.

Step 2 — Qualify each company (the 15-minute pass)

  • Engineering blog — do they write about the problems you want to work on? Read the 2 most recent technical posts; note names of authors and specific systems (great outreach hooks).
  • Tech stack — from the JD, the blog, StackShare, or their open-source. Is it adjacent to yours (Java/Spring/Postgres/Kafka), or a full retool (Go/gRPC/Rust)?
  • Compensation band — levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Wellfound. Rule it out early if it's below your floor.
  • Health & stage — funding round + date, headcount trend on LinkedIn, recent layoffs, Glassdoor trajectory (not the absolute score, the trend).
  • Interview process — Glassdoor / Leetcode discuss / interviewing.io notes. How many rounds, DSA-heavy or system-design-heavy, take-home or live.
  • The connection — do you know anyone there, or a 2nd-degree who could refer? This decides your application path.

Step 3 — Score and tier

Score each on Interest (1–5), Fit (1–5), Reachability (1–5 — comp in band + you have a referral path). Sum, then tier:

  • Tier A (11–15) — dream + reachable. Get a referral, tailor everything, apply last (after you've done a few reps).
  • Tier B (7–10) — solid. The bulk of your applications.
  • Tier C (≤6) — practice reps. Apply early to warm up your interview muscles on lower stakes.
Common mistakes
  • Applying to Tier-A companies in week one, before you've had any interview practice.
  • Researching so thoroughly you apply to 3 companies a week. Timebox it: 15 min qualify, move on.
  • Ignoring comp bands and getting deep into a process that could never clear your floor.
  • Not recording why a company was interesting — you'll need that exact detail for the cover note and the "why us?" question.

5. Cold outreach & referrals

The path is: referral > recruiter reply > cold application. Outreach is how you get the first two. Send to (in order of value) an engineer on the team, an engineering manager, then a recruiter. Keep it under 120 words, make the ask small, and never attach a resume in the first message.

The five-part template

Subject → Hook → Proof → Fit → CTA 
Subject:  Backend engineer interested in [Team] at [Company]

Hook:     I read [Name]'s post on [specific system — e.g. "your
          move to a cell-based architecture"] — the part about
          [specific detail] matched a problem I hit at [my company].

Proof:    I'm a backend engineer at [company], 3 years on
          [Java/Spring/Kafka]. Recently I [one concrete, quantified
          win — "cut checkout p99 from 900ms to 180ms"].

Fit:      I noticed you're hiring for [role]. My work on
          [domain] lines up closely with what that team does.

CTA:      Would you be open to a 15-minute call so I can learn
          more about the team? Or if it's easier, I'm happy to
          apply and reference this note.
Why it works
  • Hook proves you did homework and aren't mass-mailing — the single biggest reply-rate lever.
  • Proof is one quantified line, not your life story.
  • CTA is a small, specific ask ("15 minutes") with an even smaller fallback ("just apply and reference this").

Referral-ask variant (someone you already know)

Hi [Name] — hope [team/kids/city] is good. I'm starting a job
search focused on backend roles at product companies, and
[Company] is near the top of my list — [one sentence why].

I saw an opening for [role] ([link]). Would you be comfortable
referring me? No worries at all if not. I've attached my resume
and a 3-line blurb you can paste into the referral form to make
it zero effort.

Thanks either way — happy to return the favour anytime.

Always include the paste-ready blurb: "[Name] is a backend engineer with 3 years on Java/Spring at [company]; recently [one win]. Strong fit for the [role] opening."

9. STAR behavioral bank — worked examples

Situation (10s, context only) → Task (10s, your specific responsibility) → Action (40s, what you did, step by step) → Result (15s, quantified + what you learned). Write your own version of each prompt below; the worked answers are templates in the right voice, not scripts to memorise. Aim for 6–8 stories total that you can flex across 20+ questions.

The core story set. Most behavioral questions map to one of: (1) conflict / disagreement, (2) failure / mistake, (3) leadership / influence without authority, (4) ambiguity / ownership, (5) tight deadline / pressure, (6) technical challenge, (7) feedback received, (8) impact you're proud of. Prepare one strong story for each.

Example 1 — Conflict / disagreement with a teammate

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague."

  • S: On the payments service, a senior engineer wanted to add a second database for a new reporting feature; I thought it would create a dual-write consistency problem.
  • T: I owned the write path, so I had to either get aligned or raise it before we committed to the design.
  • A: I didn't argue it in the meeting. I built a one-page doc: the dual-write failure scenario with a concrete sequence, the on-call cost, and two alternatives — a read replica, and an outbox → analytics pipeline. I walked him through it 1:1 first, acknowledged the reporting need was real, and asked where my reasoning was wrong. He pushed back on replica lag; we agreed the outbox option handled both concerns. We took the revised design to the team together.
  • R: Shipped the outbox pipeline; zero consistency incidents in the 8 months since, and it became the pattern two other teams reused. What I learned: bring a written argument and a real alternative, and disagree in private before you disagree in public.

Example 2 — Failure / mistake

"Tell me about a time you failed."

  • S: I pushed a change to the notification service that added a new Kafka consumer group.
  • T: It was my change and my deploy; I'd tested it locally and in staging.
  • A: I missed that the consumer wasn't idempotent. Under a partition rebalance in production it reprocessed a batch and double-sent ~3,000 emails before alerts fired. I rolled back within 15 minutes, then owned it in the incident channel — no hedging. I wrote the postmortem myself: root cause was no dedupe key, contributing cause was that staging had one partition so rebalancing never happened. Fixes: a Redis SETNX dedupe on message ID, staging topics repartitioned to match prod, and a checklist item for "is this consumer idempotent?"
  • R: No recurrence. The idempotency checklist caught two would-be bugs in the next quarter during review. What I learned: staging has to match prod on the dimensions that cause failures — partition count, not just "it's the same code."

Example 3 — Leadership / influence without authority

"Tell me about a time you drove a change beyond your own work."

  • S: Our team's services had no consistent integration testing; each repo did its own thing and flaky tests were routinely ignored.
  • T: Not my assigned project — I was a mid-level engineer and this was a team-wide habit.
  • A: I picked the worst-offending repo and converted it to Testcontainers with a clean pattern: real Postgres + Kafka in the test, deterministic fixtures, <90s runtime. I wrote it up as a short internal doc and a template repo, then offered to pair with anyone migrating. I didn't mandate it — I made the good path the easy path and demoed the flaky-test reduction in a team meeting.
  • R: 5 of 7 services migrated within two months by their own owners; CI flakiness dropped from ~1 in 4 builds to under 1 in 20. What I learned: you change a team habit by building the reference implementation and lowering the switching cost, not by winning the argument.

Example 4 — Ambiguity / ownership

"Tell me about a time you had to make progress with unclear requirements."

  • S: Product asked for "faster checkout" after complaints, with no specifics and no metric.
  • T: I took it on as the backend owner of checkout and had to define the problem before solving it.
  • A: I added tracing across the checkout flow and pulled a week of latency data broken down by step. p50 was fine; p99 was 2.1s, driven by a synchronous call to the tax service and an N+1 in cart pricing. I framed three options for product with effort and expected impact, and proposed a target: p99 under 500ms. We agreed on scope in 20 minutes because now there was data.
  • A (cont.): Made the tax call async with a cached fallback, replaced the N+1 with a batch query, added a covering index.
  • R: p99 went from 2.1s to 380ms; checkout abandonment dropped ~6%. What I learned: "make it faster" is not a task — instrument first, turn the vague ask into a number, then the scope negotiation is easy.

Example 5 — Tight deadline / pressure

"Tell me about a time you were under significant time pressure."

  • S: A payment provider announced they were deprecating the API we used, with a hard cutoff 3 weeks out. Miss it and payments stop.
  • T: I was the most familiar with the integration, so I led the migration.
  • A: I cut scope hard: build an adapter interface so the rest of the code didn't change, migrate only the endpoints we actually used (6 of ~20), and defer nice-to-haves. I split the work — I took auth + capture, a teammate took webhooks + refunds. We ran both providers in parallel behind a feature flag with a 5% canary, watched reconciliation for 48 hours, then ramped. I gave the manager a daily one-line status so there were no surprises.
  • R: Fully cut over 4 days before the deadline, zero failed payments during the switch, reconciliation clean. What I learned: under a hard deadline the lever is scope, and an adapter boundary is what makes aggressive scope-cutting safe.
Common mistakes
  • Spending 40 seconds on Situation and 10 on Action. Invert it.
  • "We" everywhere — the interviewer can't tell what you did. Use "I" for your actions.
  • No result, or an unquantified one. Even "reduced escalations noticeably" beats trailing off.
  • No learning. Every story ends with one sentence of what you'd carry forward.
  • A "failure" that's secretly a brag ("I work too hard"). Pick a real one with a real fix.
  • Picking a conflict story where you were clearly right and they were clearly an idiot. Show you can be moved by evidence.

10. Salary negotiation scripts

Negotiation starts at the recruiter screen, not the offer. Two principles: never name a number first if you can avoid it, and never accept or reject on the call — "thank you, I'm excited, I'd like to review the details and come back by [date]." Everything below is polite, specific, and leaves room.

The recruiter asks for your current CTC

Deflect — you're not obliged to share it

"I'd prefer to keep my current compensation private, but I'm happy to share expectations so we don't waste each other's time. Based on my research for this role and level in [city], I'm targeting a total comp in the range of [X to X+25%]. Does that work with the band for this role?"

If they insist it's required by policy: give a total-comp number including variable and the current value of any stock, and immediately pivot to expectations. In several jurisdictions asking is restricted — you can simply say you'd rather discuss the future role.

The recruiter pushes for an expected-CTC number

Give a researched range, anchored high, with a reason

"From levels.fyi and conversations with engineers at similar companies, this role at this level looks like [₹Y – ₹Y+30%] total comp. I'm interviewing across a few companies in that band. If the role and team are a strong fit, I'd expect an offer in that range."

  • Always a range, and make your walk-away number the bottom of it.
  • Total comp — base + variable + stock (annualised) — not just base.
  • Name that you have other processes going, without bluffing specifics you can't back.

Handling a lowball offer

Don't counter with a number yet — get the gap acknowledged

"Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about the team and the [specific project]. I want to be straightforward: the offer is meaningfully below what I'm seeing for this role and level, and below my other conversations. I was expecting total comp closer to [₹Z]. Is there flexibility to get there? I'd like to make this work."

Then stop talking. If they ask what would close it:

"A base of [₹B] and [₹E] in equity would get me to yes. If base is capped, I'm open to closing the gap with a signing bonus or a 6-month review commitment."

  • Ask for a specific, justified number — not "more."
  • Give them levers: base, equity, sign-on, review timeline, joining date, title.
  • Stay warm. "I want to make this work" every time.

Negotiating with a competing offer

Use it as information, not a threat

"I want to be transparent — I have another offer at [₹total]. You're my first choice because of [specific, honest reason], but the gap is significant. If you can get to [₹target] total, I'll sign and stop my other processes today."

  • Only mention a competing offer if it's real. Bluffing collapses the moment they call it.
  • Give a concrete "if you hit X, I sign today" — a decision, not a bidding war.
  • If the other offer is from a stronger-brand company, you don't even need the number — "a company with a higher comp band" is enough.
  • Never play games with your first choice past one round. Get to yes and stop.
Common mistakes
  • Accepting on the phone out of relief. Always take 24–72 hours.
  • Negotiating base only — equity, sign-on, and level are often more movable.
  • Apologising for negotiating. It's expected; recruiters rarely revoke over a polite counter.
  • Not getting the final offer in writing before resigning anywhere.
  • Negotiating without a real walk-away number decided in advance.

11. Offer evaluation framework

Compare offers on total compensation over a realistic horizon (usually 4 years), then adjust for risk and the non-money factors. Break every offer into four buckets:

  • Base — guaranteed cash. Weight this highest; it's certain and it's what raises and future offers anchor on.
  • Variable / bonus — target %, but discount it: historical payout ratio, company performance. Assume 80–90% unless you know better.
  • Stock — RSUs: grant value ÷ vest years, but haircut private-company equity heavily (illiquid, may be worth zero). Public RSUs: use current price, ignore hoped-for growth. ESOPs with a strike price: usually treat as a lottery ticket, not comp.
  • Benefits & conditions — insurance value, PF, meal/transport, learning budget, WFH (a real ₹ saving), commute time, on-call load, sabbatical, notice period, joining bonus (one-time — separate it out).
Core intuition

A big number that's 60% illiquid private equity is not the same as a slightly smaller number that's 90% base. Convert everything to expected, risk-adjusted annual cash before you compare, and keep one-time bonuses out of the recurring figure.

Non-money checklist before you sign

  • Did you talk to your future manager and at least one peer? Would you want them in a code review?
  • What will you actually work on in the first 6 months — concretely?
  • On-call rotation frequency and severity.
  • For a startup: months of runway, last raise date & amount, burn, path to next round or profitability.
  • Level / title — does it set you up for the role after this one?
  • Notice period, WFH policy in writing, relocation terms, joining-bonus clawback period.
  • Full offer letter reviewed in writing before you resign anywhere.
